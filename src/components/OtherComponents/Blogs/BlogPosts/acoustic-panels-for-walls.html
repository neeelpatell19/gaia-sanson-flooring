Most modern rooms are built from materials that reflect sound: glass, concrete, plaster, stone and tile. They look clean and last for years, and they make rooms echo. Acoustic wall panels are the simplest way to put absorption back into a room without changing its shape, and they have moved from studios and auditoriums into offices, restaurants, schools and living rooms. This page covers what they are, the types available, how to choose and where to put them, and links to our detailed articles on each step.

Acoustic wall panels at a glance

  • What they do: absorb sound inside a room, reducing echo and noise build-up.
  • What they do not do: stop sound reaching the room next door.
  • What decides performance: material, thickness and mounting.
  • Where they go: wherever voices strike first, spread around the room.
  • How they look: plain, grooved or printed, as quiet or as bold as the room needs.

What are acoustic wall panels?

Panels made from a porous material that lets sound in and turns part of its energy into a tiny amount of heat. Each time a sound wave strikes the panel, less of it bounces back into the room, so speech fades faster and noise does not pile up. That makes a room easier to talk in and less tiring to spend time in. Panels are not soundproofing: blocking sound between rooms needs mass and airtight construction, as soundproofing compared with absorption explains.

Which types of acoustic wall panels are there?

  • PET felt panels: rigid boards of polyester fibre, cut to shape and fixed flat or on a gap. Durable, light and available in many colours.
  • Grooved and patterned panels: PET boards with carved grooves or cut patterns that add relief and texture.
  • Printed panels: PET boards carrying artwork, murals or branding on the face.
  • Slatted timber panels: timber or veneer slats over an absorbent backing, popular in homes and hospitality.
  • Fabric-wrapped panels: a fibrous core wrapped in acoustic fabric, common in auditoriums and studios.
  • Acoustic foam: soft, lightweight and inexpensive, mostly used in studios and hidden areas; see how it compares in panels versus foam.

How well do acoustic panels absorb sound?

It depends on the panel and especially on how it is mounted. Absorption is measured in a reverberation room under ASTM C423 or ISO 354, and summarised as a single number: NRC in the Indian and American market, or an absorption class from A (best) to E under ISO 11654 in Europe. Glued flat, a GAIA PET panel rates NRC 0.3; set forward on a cavity, the same board rates NRC 0.85–0.90. When comparing products, always compare figures tested in the same mounting you plan to use. Which thickness should you choose?

The thicker the board and the deeper the space behind it, the lower the frequencies it can absorb. Thin panels mainly tame high, sharp sounds such as clatter and sibilance; thicker panels and cavities reach further into the low, booming range of voices in a full room. As a rough guide, thin panels suit decorative treatment and small rooms, while thicker panels on a gap suit meeting rooms, restaurants and any room where speech must stay clear. Where should acoustic panels go on a wall?

Where sound strikes first and most often. In practice:

  • Around head height. A band covering seated and standing head height catches direct speech.
  • On facing walls. Treat both of any pair of parallel hard walls, staggering the panels so reflections cannot bounce cleanly between them.
  • In groups, not one block. Several groups spread around the room work better than the same area concentrated on one wall.
  • Opposite glass. Glass cannot be treated, so the wall facing it needs extra absorption.
  • With the ceiling. In tall rooms, part of the area belongs overhead as clouds or baffles.

How are acoustic wall panels installed?

Either flat against the wall with adhesive or mechanical fixings, or on battens, clips or a frame that leaves an air gap behind. Direct fixing is quicker and slimmer; an air gap absorbs far more. Either way, switches, sockets, lights and vents must be planned on the wall elevation before any panel is cut. Our step-by-step article on installing acoustic panels covers both methods.

Which colours and finishes work best?

The ones that suit the room, because performance comes from the board, not its colour. Deep colours make a panelled wall recede and frame a screen or artwork; pale colours keep a small room bright; a single strong accent turns a necessary acoustic wall into a feature. Grooved panels add relief that catches light along a corridor or behind a reception desk, and printed panels let the wall carry a logo, a mural or a pattern. In rooms used for video calls, choose calm, mid-tone finishes behind people's heads rather than busy patterns, which can distract on camera.

Acoustic wall panel questions

Do acoustic panels have to cover the whole wall?

No. Most rooms need treatment on part of several walls rather than all of one. Spreading panels in groups is more effective than covering a single wall completely.

How do you clean PET acoustic panels?

Dust them with a soft brush or a vacuum fitted with a brush attachment. Keep water and cleaning sprays off the face unless the supplier confirms they are safe.

Can acoustic panels go in kitchens, bathrooms or outdoors?

Keep standard acoustic panels to dry, indoor rooms. Steam, splashes, grease and rain all affect porous materials, so wet areas and covered outdoor spaces need products and fixings specified for those conditions. Ask our team before planning panels anywhere damp.

What thickness of acoustic panel is best for walls?

For offices, classrooms and feature walls, 9 or 12 mm usually does the job, ideally on an air gap. For home theatres, studios and loud halls, 18 or 24 mm boards control low sound better.
