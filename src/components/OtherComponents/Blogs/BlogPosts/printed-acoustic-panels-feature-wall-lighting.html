A printed wall panel has two jobs. It has to hold an image well enough to become the focal point of a room, and it has to absorb some of the noise that bare plaster and glass bounce around. Lighting decides how well it does the first. Fixing decides the second. Most disappointing feature walls go wrong on one of those two decisions, rarely on the artwork itself.

The catalogue lists them in 9 mm, 12 mm, 18 mm and 24 mm thicknesses, with patterns that can be customised to the brief. What follows is how to plan the light, the fixing and the artwork together so the wall reads as intended once the room is finished.

Fixing comes first, because it changes the light

For its PET acoustic panels, GAIA publishes two very different sound absorption figures: NRC 0.3 when a panel is fixed directly to the wall, and NRC 0.85–0.90 when it is installed with an air gap behind it. Confirm the figures for the printed panel and thickness you select, but the principle holds for planning. If the wall's acoustic job matters, the panel is likely to stand off the wall.

That depth has consequences the lighting designer needs to know about early:

  • Shadow lines. A panel mounted proud of the wall will throw a shadow along its lower edge under a downlight placed close to the wall. On a single framed panel this can look deliberate. Across a run of panels it can read as a stripe.
  • Sconces and switches. Wall lights, switches and sockets inside the panel area need cut-outs or relocation. Their positions, and the cable routes behind the panel, should be fixed before sheets are cut.
  • Heat and clearance. Keep fittings that run warm at a sensible distance from the panel face. Confirm clearances with GAIA and the fitting manufacturer rather than assuming them, and ask GAIA for the fire test documentation your project requires.

Wash the print; save grazing for texture

Two lighting approaches come up for feature walls, and they suit different surfaces.

Wall washing uses fittings set back from the wall to spread light evenly from top to bottom. It shows colour and pattern as printed, with minimal shadow, which is usually what a printed panel needs. Recessed wall washers, a track with wide-beam spots or a concealed linear fitting in the ceiling can all do this. What matters is even coverage without a hot band near the ceiling.

Grazing places the fitting very close to the wall so light rakes across the surface. It is excellent at revealing relief, which is why it suits carved or grooved acoustic panels. On a flat printed panel it tends to emphasise the wrong things: panel joints, fixings and any slight unevenness in the wall behind.

Whichever you choose, the fitting and the panel remain separate systems. The panel does not house or conceal lighting, so drivers, access for maintenance and dimming belong to the electrical design.

Colour under your light, not the showroom's

Saturated prints are the least forgiving. A strong blue or green can look dull under a lamp with weak colour rendering, and warm light will push a cool palette towards grey. Two checks save most of the trouble:

  • specify fittings with good colour rendering for the feature wall, even if the rest of the room uses a general-purpose lamp;
  • look at a printed sample on site, under the actual fitting and colour temperature, in daylight and after dark.

Pattern scale deserves the same test. A fine plaid or thin wavy line that reads crisply at arm's length can blur into a flat tone from across a café or an open office. Large, simple shapes hold up better at distance and under softer light.

Plan the artwork around the sheet

A continuous mural wider than one 1220 × 2440 mm sheet will cross panel joints. Decide where those joints fall before the artwork is finalised, so that faces, lettering and logos do not break across them. It is also the moment to agree the artwork file requirements with GAIA, since resolution and format depend on the final print size.

For branded walls in receptions and meeting rooms, it is worth checking the logo colours on a printed sample against the brand guidelines. Printing onto a fibrous acoustic surface is not the same as printing onto paper or vinyl.

What to send GAIA

A useful first brief for a printed acoustic feature wall includes:

  • the wall elevation with dimensions, and any doors, windows or columns;
  • the artwork, pattern reference or brand files;
  • the lighting layout, with fitting types and positions near the wall;
  • switches, sockets and wall lights within the panel area;
  • how much the room's acoustics matter, which will guide thickness and whether an air gap is needed.

With that, the team can advise on panel thickness, layout, joints and fixing, and you can coordinate the lighting in parallel. The wider GAIA acoustic range includes grooved and perforated panels for walls where texture matters more than image.

Questions that come up often

Can printed acoustic panels be lit with a wall washer?

Yes. Wall washing is usually the better choice for a printed panel because it shows colour and pattern evenly. Fitting positions, clearances and cable routes should be coordinated with the panel layout before installation.

Do printed acoustic panels still absorb sound?

They are acoustic PET panels, so absorption depends largely on thickness and fixing. GAIA publishes NRC 0.3 for its PET panels fixed directly and NRC 0.85–0.90 with an air gap; confirm the figures for your chosen printed panel.
